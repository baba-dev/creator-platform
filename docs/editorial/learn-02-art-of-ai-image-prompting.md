Two images can show exactly the same object and tell completely different
stories. In one, a cobalt-blue ceramic cup sits in the centre of a quiet frame.
In another, it catches a hard stripe of late-afternoon light. In a third, the
cup is small, surrounded by generous space for a campaign headline. The subject
has not changed. The direction has.

That is the skill this guide develops: using an AI image generator as an
art-direction tool, not an adjective machine. The first guide in our series
introduced the complete prompt-to-final workflow. Here we slow down and learn
the visual choices inside a great prompt: composition, light, colour, style, and
continuity across a collection of images.

> A better image usually begins with a better decision about what the viewer
> should notice, feel, and remember.

## One object, many possible stories

We will work with one deliberately simple example: a handmade cobalt-blue
ceramic coffee cup, with a rounded handle and a slightly irregular glazed rim.
There is no logo, no readable text, and no distracting second product. Keeping
the object consistent makes the effect of each creative decision easier to see.

Imagine that a small design-led café wants three campaign assets: a calm website
hero, an atmospheric social post, and a product-led announcement. It would be
wasteful to invent three unrelated worlds. Instead, we can design one visual
system and vary its expression for each placement.

The illustrations throughout this guide are editorial diagrams, not benchmark
outputs from a particular image model. Their purpose is to make the creative
decisions visible; actual generated results will depend on the model, source
references, and settings you choose.

## Composition: tell the eye where to go

Composition is how visual elements occupy a frame. Before adding texture, camera
jargon, or dramatic colour, ask a simpler question: where should the viewer look
first? Subject size, placement, directional lines, and empty space all influence
the answer.

### Centre the subject when stillness matters

Centred framing can feel confident and calm, particularly for a product that
needs a simple, recognisable silhouette. Ask for a single cup centred against a
quiet background, with a balanced horizon and no competing objects. Symmetry is
a choice, not a rule: a small asymmetry in a handmade object can keep an
otherwise composed frame human.

### Use thirds to create a relationship

Placing the hero near one third of the canvas leaves room for the environment to
participate. For our café scene, the cup might sit in the right third while a
soft window shadow gives the left side atmosphere. Mention the relationship
explicitly rather than relying on the phrase “rule of thirds” alone.

### Design negative space for real layouts

Negative space is not wasted space. It is the breathable region that can later
hold a headline, a price, a button, or simply a pause. Request the cup on the
right with the left half free of important detail. Do not ask the model to
invent the final marketing copy inside the photograph; place critical typography
in your design or editing workflow afterward.

![Three editorial diagrams of the same blue ceramic cup: centred, aligned to a third, and shifted to leave large negative space for copy](/learn-assets/image-art-direction/composition-study.png)

_One subject, three different jobs. These schematic frames show why composition
should be decided before production._

**Try this controlled test:** Keep the cup, background, and light unchanged.
Generate a centred version, a thirds-based version, and a version with empty
space on the left. Compare usefulness, not just beauty. A hero image must still
work after the site headline is added.

## Lighting: give the scene an emotional temperature

Lighting reveals surfaces, creates depth, and changes perceived mood. “Cinematic
lighting” is a vague request. A direction such as “soft daylight entering from
frame left, a gentle highlight along the glazed rim, and a long diffuse shadow
to the right” explains what the image should actually show.

### Soft side light: warmth and material

For a welcoming café campaign, ask for broad morning light from a nearby window.
A soft edge on the shadow allows the slightly uneven glaze and ceramic texture
to remain visible. The image feels approachable because the contrast is
controlled.

### Overcast diffusion: honest detail

Diffuse light is useful when the product shape and finish matter more than
atmosphere. Ask for even illumination, restrained reflections, and an
uncluttered neutral background. This is a good direction for a catalogue-like
supporting image, though it may not be the most expressive campaign cover.

### Rim light: silhouette and drama

For a more graphic social image, move the scene toward blue hour and introduce a
narrow edge light. The cup separates from the darker background, while the light
picks out its handle and rim. This direction can be striking, but do not let
contrast erase important product details.

![Three stylised comparisons of the same blue cup in warm side light, diffuse overcast light, and dramatic blue-hour light](/learn-assets/image-art-direction/lighting-study.png)

_Change the light, not the object. Each treatment serves a different emotional
and commercial purpose._

When a result feels flat, inspect the actual highlights and shadows before
adding ten more style adjectives. Does the main light have a believable
direction? Is the handle readable? Are the reflections consistent with the
scene? Correct the evidence you can see.

## Colour and style: create a system, not a pile of adjectives

Style becomes more useful when you can describe its ingredients. “Modern premium
editorial” could mean a limited palette, tactile materials, confident cropping,
calm spacing, and intentional contrast. A string of labels such as modern,
dreamy, luxury, cinematic, minimalist and hyperreal can pull the generator in
competing directions.

Give your project a small visual vocabulary. For our cup campaign, begin with
cobalt blue as the anchor, chalky cream for the environment, warm sand for
contrast, and a muted dark ink for shadow. Let the cup remain the strongest
saturated shape. Repeating these relationships helps separate a campaign from a
collection of unrelated pretty pictures.

### Write a miniature style sheet

- **Anchor:** handmade cobalt-blue ceramic cup, rounded handle, subtly irregular
  glazed rim.

- **Palette:** cobalt, chalk, warm sand, dark ink; avoid unrelated neon accents.

- **Materials:** tactile stone, paper, soft fabric, natural ceramic reflections.

- **Camera language:** quiet editorial framing, legible silhouette, purposeful
  crop.

- **Lighting language:** one primary direction per frame, controlled highlights,
  believable shadows.

- **Exclusions:** no extra cups, no text, no fictitious logo, no watermark.

This is a creative-direction sheet, not a promise that all models will perfectly
obey every request. It provides an anchor against which outputs can be reviewed
and corrected.

## Consistency: how to make images feel like one campaign

Consistency has at least three separate dimensions. **Subject consistency** asks
whether the object stays recognisable. **World consistency** asks whether the
materials, colours, and atmosphere belong together. **Output consistency** asks
whether the final assets have compatible crops, contrast, and production
quality. A series can fail even when each image looks attractive on its own.

For the cup, write down the non-negotiable facts: cobalt-blue glaze, rounded
handle, uneven handmade lip, one cup only. Hold those steady. If the selected
model supports reference images, provide a clean image you own or are permitted
to use. A reference can help anchor appearance, but the prompt still has to
direct the composition and environment. Reference capabilities vary by model,
and neither text nor a reference guarantees exact fidelity.

### The anchor-and-variation method

1. Create or select one strong anchor frame that matches the brief.

2. Record its defining attributes in a short visual style sheet.

3. Keep the subject description and any suitable reference unchanged.

4. Change a single intended variable: composition, light, background, or crop.

5. Compare results against the anchor and keep only the variations that belong
   together.

A familiar mistake is rewriting the entire prompt after a near miss. That
destroys the experiment. If the product is right but the background is too
crowded, preserve the product direction and change the background instruction.
If the light is strong but the handle is wrong, correct the product shape or
reference rather than replacing the whole scene.

## Three ready-to-adapt art-direction prompts

The following prompts are deliberately related. They share a visual identity
while solving different communication tasks. Copy them as starting points, then
adapt the object, audience, format, and model-supported options to your project.

### 01 — A calm website hero

```text
Editorial product photograph of one handmade cobalt-blue ceramic coffee cup, rounded handle and subtly irregular glazed rim, on a pale limestone table. Cup placed in the right third of a wide composition, with generous uncluttered negative space on the left for a website headline. Soft morning window light from frame left, tactile natural surfaces, restrained cobalt, chalk and warm-sand palette, quiet believable reflections. No second cup, no people, no logo, no readable text, no watermark.
```

**Why it works:** The job, object, placement, and layout requirement are
explicit. The background supports the product instead of competing with it.

### 02 — An atmospheric social frame

```text
One handmade cobalt-blue ceramic coffee cup with the same rounded handle and slightly irregular glazed rim, on a warm stone surface at blue hour. Closer vertical editorial framing, cup occupying the lower centre, deep indigo background, a precise warm rim light outlining the ceramic edge, controlled shadow detail and a small area of breathing room above. Consistent cobalt, chalk and warm-sand art direction. No extra products, no lettering, no logos, no watermark.
```

**Why it works:** The product identity remains, while the lighting and crop
deliberately change the mood. Check the available aspect ratios in your chosen
model before generating.

### 03 — An informative product-detail image

```text
Close editorial product photograph of the same handmade cobalt-blue ceramic coffee cup, prioritising the rounded handle, tactile glaze and subtly uneven rim. Neutral chalk-coloured background, soft diffuse daylight, true-to-material texture, clean silhouette, minimal props and enough separation between cup and background to inspect the form. Keep colour and product details consistent with the campaign reference. No invented branding, no text, no second cup, no watermark.
```

**Why it works:** The image serves examination rather than atmosphere. It
belongs to the same campaign without pretending that every asset should have
identical lighting or framing.

## Put the method to work in Image Studio

Open Image Studio with your creative brief and one of the example prompts.
Select a model based on the capabilities your task needs. Some models offer
references, different output counts, aspect ratios, or resolutions; the
available controls should follow the model you select. Use Enhance Prompt as an
editing aid, then review the result against the style sheet rather than treating
an expanded prompt as automatically better.

Choose the intended canvas before you generate. Where supported, add a suitable
reference to preserve your subject. Review the credit and OMR estimate for the
selected configuration, then make your first purposeful comparison. Keep the
promising result in the Asset Library, and use Precision Image Studio for
supported finishing tasks such as cropping, resizing, and layout preparation.

Do not use a completed image as proof that every detail is accurate. Review
geometry, product identity, text-like artefacts, shadow logic, and whether a
design still works at its final display size. If you need exact packaging or
brand lettering, place verified design elements in the finishing workflow
instead of relying on generated spelling.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can one prompt produce a consistent visual series?

One carefully structured base prompt helps, but consistency also depends on the
model, references, controlled variables, and human review. Treat the series as
an art-directed process, not a one-click guarantee.

### Is more detailed prompting always better?

No. Detail helps when it resolves ambiguity. Instructions that duplicate or
contradict each other can make your direction harder to interpret. Keep the
brief specific and the visual hierarchy clear.

### Should I put text inside my image prompt?

If accurate copy matters, reserve space for it and add verified typography
during layout or editing. Generated lettering may be unreliable and can
complicate revision.

### How can I tell whether a style is consistent?

Lay the frames next to each other. Check the subject, colour relationships,
material treatment, light logic, contrast, and quality at actual usage sizes. A
cohesive series should look related without being identical.

## Your five-minute creative-direction exercise

Choose one object you can describe precisely. Write down three non-negotiable
traits. Create one calm composition, then produce two controlled alternatives:
change only the framing in one and only the lighting in the other. Place all
three side by side, and write one sentence about what each image communicates.
This is how taste becomes a repeatable decision process.

> Do not ask only whether the picture is beautiful. Ask whether it belongs to
> the story you are trying to tell.

When you are ready, take the calm website-hero prompt into Image Studio, adapt
it for your own subject, and build a visual system that can grow with your next
campaign.
